** Big Structural Problems   
<<m-bsp>>

*** Church switch
<<m-bsp1>>

1. The Jesus/Sanhedrin pivot is the single biggest risk.

The transition happens here: "I look outside. The churches. Both of them. Wouldn't mind them, if their operators hadn't perverted Christianity."

Up to this point, the reader has been with a slightly cranky, slightly lonely man having recognizable thoughts about a waitress — boobs, age, kids, piercings. These are coarse but legible thoughts. Then, in the space of about two paragraphs, you launch into a full historical-political theory about ancient Israel, Rome, AIPAC, Russian oligarchs, and Epstein islands. There is no bridge. The narrator looks out a window and the reader is suddenly reading a political pamphlet.

This is the place a SubStack reader is most likely to close the tab. Not because the content is offensive — they signed up for weird — but because the narrator they were reading seems to have been replaced by a different narrator. It reads less like a drunk man's monologue (which has its own drift-logic) and more like two different documents stapled together.

The fix doesn't have to be much. Even one sentence of internal motion — something like the narrator catching himself, noticing he's doing it again, half-apologizing to the reader — would re-anchor the voice. Right now the churches are just there in the window, and then suddenly they unlock a thousand-word essay.

*** PTSD switch
<<m-bsp2>>

2. The "PTSD / experiment" line is dropped and never recovered.

Early on you write: "What will happen to my PTSD, if I spent a year or two in near-paradisic circumstances?"

This is a terrific hook. It promises the reader a specific frame: a damaged man running an experiment on himself. But the story never returns to it explicitly. By the end, when the narrator is planning arson, the reader has no easy way to connect "the experiment failed" or "the experiment worked but produced this" or "the PTSD is what's actually talking now." The line just sits there unredeemed.

This matters structurally because the PTSD line is the element that could make the arson ending feel inevitable in retrospect rather than tacked on. Right now you're not cashing in the chip you set up.

*** The ask-her-out question dies without resolution
<<m-bsp3>>

3. The ask-her-out question dies without resolution.

The narrator spends real estate on "should I ask her out?" — debating her age, her piercing, her fertility. Then the question is simply abandoned. He decides to commit arson and walks out. The romantic question doesn't get a no; it gets forgotten.

For your stated arc to work — reader expects romance question to resolve, gets arson instead — the romance question needs to be answered by the arson decision, not just displaced by it. Right now it feels less like "he chose arson over her" and more like "the writer changed subjects." There's one line near the end that gestures at this ("You lose the chance to date this woman. You gain the chance to prevent the extermination of millions...") but it arrives after the reader has already been doing political theory for a while, so it lands as an afterthought rather than as the resolution of the original tension.

I am writing a short story in near future, dystopian Russia.

The government is about to pass a bill which will allow to implant every Russian citizen with a `Neuralink`-like sensor which wirelessly connects to a mobile phone running the infamous `Max` messenger. The `Max` messenger would get the ability to read and write brain signals via that `Neuralink` device.

The bill is public, but written in a way that its implications cannot be deduced from a complicated legal text.

Assume there is a main character, some legal tech geek living in the remotest possible part of Russia. This guy notices the bill and discovers how harmful it will be, if it is passed (contrary to everybody else -- because the wording of the bill conceals its harm).

He writes a text in which he explains that if the bill is passed (which is likely to happen 1-7 days from now) then the government will be allowed to snatch any person on the street and install that `Neuralink`-device into the brain. Even if the carrier of the implant does not have a `Max`-infected phone on him or her, that `Neuralink`-device will connect to any other `Max`-infefted phone in the vicinity (which is likely because all mobile phones now have to have that malware named `Max` to be installed).

Assume there is a group of people scattered all around Russia who are active enough to do legal, non-violent, solitary (i. e. no demonstrations) things to resist the government.

That group reads the protagonist's writeup and does `SMSCI` (something the state can't ignore) which prevents the government from passing the bill.

Here I use `state` and `government` as synonyms.

`SMSCI` are legal, non-violent, solitary actions which give the state a negative feedback (i. e. make the state realize that the population is not content with something the government is doing or about to do).

`SMSCI` include actions like the obvious ones:

1. Write to a senator (member of the Russian upper house).
2. Write to a deputy (member of the Russian lower house).
3. Issue a petition signed by many people.

What are other actions a Russian citizen can do that satisfy the following criteria?

1. The action sends a negative feedback signal to the state (government).
2. The action is legal.
3. The action is non-violent.
4. The action is scalable (many Russian citizens can do it).

Note: Because I need this for a fictional story, I can bend the reality if it fits the story.

** 53 (2026-06-13 13:34): Citizen appeal
<<n53>>

A citizen can file a formal /obrashchenie/ (citizen appeal) under Federal Law No. 59-FZ "On the procedure for considering appeals of citizens." This is a powerful and underused mechanism: the state is legally obligated to register and respond within 30 days. A coordinated flood of tens of thousands of individually-filed appeals about a specific bill creates a paper trail the bureaucracy cannot legally ignore — it must process each one. This is arguably your single best "SMSCI" device, because the obligation to respond is statutory.
